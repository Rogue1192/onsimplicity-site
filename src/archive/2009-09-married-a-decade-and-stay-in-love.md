<p>A decade of marriage can contain several versions of a life: different jobs, changing bodies, children or the decision not to have them, illness, moves, losses, and ordinary weeks that blur together. Remaining close through those changes involves more than preserving the atmosphere of an early date.</p>
<p>“Madly in love” need not mean feeling intensely romantic every day. It can mean continuing to enjoy each other, treating the relationship as worth attention, and making affection visible even when energy is limited. No set of habits guarantees a lasting marriage, but practical choices can support a respectful, connected one.</p>
<h2>Keep learning who your partner is now</h2>
<p>Familiarity makes life easier, but it can also encourage assumptions. A preference learned years ago becomes a permanent fact. A partner's old ambition keeps appearing in conversation after their interests have moved elsewhere.</p>
<p>Ask questions that leave room for change. What is taking the most energy lately? What would make next month feel easier? Is there something you want more time for? Listen for the answer rather than using the question as a doorway to your own concern.</p>
<p>Follow up on details. Asking how a difficult conversation went shows that the earlier discussion remained with you. If your partner says they no longer enjoy an old routine, investigate alternatives instead of treating the change as disloyalty to your shared history.</p>
<p>Curiosity should not become monitoring. Partners remain entitled to privacy, individual friendships, and thoughts that do not need to be reported. Knowing someone well is different from demanding access to everything.</p>
<h2>Make affection recognizable to its recipient</h2>
<p>A loving intention can miss its target. One partner arranges an elaborate evening while the other most wants help with a tiring morning. Someone offers solutions when the request was for a patient listener.</p>
<p>Ask directly what kinds of care feel good. That may include a greeting at the door, a warm message during the day, uninterrupted conversation, or welcome physical affection. Preferences can change with stress, health, and circumstances, so an old answer need not settle the question forever.</p>
<p>Use small gestures frequently enough that warmth is not reserved for anniversaries. The ideas in <a href="/2009/03/37-easy-ways-to-say-i-love-you/">expressing love through ordinary actions</a> can help, provided the gesture suits your partner and does not create extra work.</p>
<p>Touch and sex require ongoing consent, including within marriage. Talk about comfort, desire, and differences without treating affection as a debt. A period of reduced sexual interest is a reason for a respectful conversation, not permission for pressure or accusation.</p>
<h2>Make the shared workload genuinely shared</h2>
<p>Romance has difficulty flourishing when one person feels like the permanent household manager. Visible chores are only part of the work. Someone must notice missing supplies, schedule appointments, remember school forms, and anticipate the next obligation.</p>
<p>List recurring responsibilities together, including planning and follow-through. Assign ownership where useful, so one person is not required to delegate every step. Owning laundry, for example, means noticing when it needs doing and returning clean clothes to use, not waiting for repeated reminders.</p>
<p>Fair does not always mean identical. Disability, paid work, caregiving, and changing capacity affect what each person can do. Discuss the reasons for an arrangement and when it should be reviewed. Avoid assuming that an emergency division of labor has become a permanent agreement.</p>
<p>A brief practical meeting can keep logistics from consuming every conversation. Choose the upcoming decisions, make the necessary arrangements, and stop. Leave some time together that is not devoted to administering the household.</p>
<h2>Disagree about a problem without attacking a person</h2>
<p>Long relationships do not eliminate disagreement. A more useful goal is to make disagreement safer and clearer. Describe a specific event, explain its effect, and make a concrete request. “Please tell me before inviting guests” gives someone more to work with than “You never think about me.”</p>
<p>Stay with one subject where possible. Bringing every past frustration into a discussion about tonight's plans makes resolution difficult. If a recurring pattern matters, name it carefully and discuss it at a time when both people can participate.</p>
<p>When the conversation becomes too heated, take an agreed pause and specify when to return. A pause used to recover is different from disappearing indefinitely or using silence as punishment. Return to the concern rather than pretending that cooling down resolved it.</p>
<p>Repair needs more than the word sorry. Name what you did, acknowledge its effect without arguing it away, and explain what will change. Then follow through. Accepting an apology does not require instantly restoring trust or abandoning a reasonable boundary.</p>
<h2>Protect the couple without erasing the individuals</h2>
<p>Shared time often disappears because it looks less urgent than work and other people's requests. Put a modest plan on the calendar: breakfast together, a walk, a card game, or a conversation after dinner. Make the plan easy enough to repeat.</p>
<p>Outside boundaries can protect that time. <a href="/2009/02/15-graceful-ways-to-say-no/">Declining invitations without unnecessary guilt</a> can help when the calendar is crowded. However, protecting the marriage should never mean isolating either partner from friends, family, or support.</p>
<p>Independent interests also give a relationship room to breathe. Time apart need not signal rejection. Discuss the practical implications so one person's leisure does not automatically become the other's unpaid responsibility.</p>
<p>Occasional novelty can be simple: cook an unfamiliar dish together, explore a different neighborhood, or exchange favorite music. The aim is shared attention, not an expensive attempt to reproduce a honeymoon.</p>
<h2>Keep expectations honest, including expectations of marriage</h2>
<p>A partner cannot supply every kind of companionship, solve every insecurity, or know an unspoken need. Asking clearly is usually kinder than testing whether someone will guess. At the same time, reasonable expectations of honesty and respect should not be dismissed as demanding.</p>
<p>The distinction in <a href="/2008/12/lower-expectations/">lowering unrealistic predictions while keeping important standards</a> is especially useful here. An imperfect weekend is normal. Persistent contempt, intimidation, coercion, or control is not an ordinary romantic disappointment.</p>
<p>If both partners want support, a qualified couples professional may help with recurring conflict. Where there is abuse or fear, prioritize individual safety and confidential specialist support; joint counseling may not be appropriate. Staying married is not more important than being safe.</p>
<p>For an ordinary check-in, choose one question: what would help you feel more supported this week? Each partner can offer one answer and one realistic action. The <a href="/category/relationships/">Relationships articles</a> offer further ways to practice consideration, but the essential work remains specific to the two people involved: notice, discuss, adjust, and keep choosing respectful care.</p>
