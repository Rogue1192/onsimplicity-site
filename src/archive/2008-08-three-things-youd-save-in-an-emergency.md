<p>“What three things would you save?” can be a revealing question when asked safely over a cup of tea. It is not a checklist to complete during a fire or another immediate threat. In a real emergency, get yourself and other people to safety, follow emergency instructions, and do not delay escape or re-enter danger for belongings.</p>
<p>The useful version of the question happens beforehand. It asks which parts of your life are difficult to replace and how you can protect them now. Instead of planning a dramatic rescue of three objects, think in three groups: what supports life, what supports recovery, and what preserves meaning.</p>
<h2>First: the essentials people depend on</h2>
<p>Consider medication information, necessary assistive equipment, communication needs, and the practical requirements of children or other dependents. Work out what needs to be ready and accessible before an emergency. Your local emergency management guidance can help you tailor supplies and arrangements to the hazards where you live.</p>
<p>Make a household plan that includes pets. Keep suitable carriers or leads accessible and know who is responsible when circumstances allow safe evacuation. Never make a plan that requires a person to enter an unsafe building to search for an animal or a bag. Preparation should reduce dangerous decisions, not create more of them.</p>
<h2>Second: the information needed to rebuild</h2>
<p>Ask what you would need if you could not enter your home tomorrow. Important contacts, identification details, insurance information, and relevant medical records may matter more than many expensive objects. Store appropriate copies securely and make sure a trusted person knows how to access what is necessary.</p>
<p>Protect privacy as well as access. A folder full of sensitive documents should not sit openly in an unlocked car. For digital records, use suitable account security and a backup arrangement you understand. Test that a backup can actually be opened; owning a drive is not the same as having recoverable files.</p>
<h2>Third: the memories you would miss</h2>
<p>A letter, a family photograph, or a small handmade object may hold more meaning than furniture. Name a few irreplaceable items while there is no urgency. Photograph or scan what can be copied, and keep copies somewhere separate from the originals. Ask permission before sharing private family material.</p>
<p>This reflection can also make <a href="/2008/09/three-things-you-bought-or-received-and-never-use/">decisions about unused possessions</a> clearer. Some objects preserve a relationship or memory; others persist mainly because nobody has decided where they belong. You can value the first group without treating every cupboard as a vault.</p>
<h2>Turn reflection into preparation</h2>
<p>Choose a few ordinary actions: update an emergency contact, organize a document, check an evacuation route, or arrange a backup. Keep exits clear and make sure household members understand the plan. Review arrangements when someone moves, medication changes, or a new pet joins the home.</p>
<p>The perspective behind <a href="/2008/06/why-choose-simplicity/">choosing a simpler life</a> is helpful here: fewer unresolved decisions can leave more attention for what matters. That does not mean safety is a reward for being organized. Emergencies happen in every kind of home, and no collection of belongings is worth risking a life.</p>
<h2>Plan for being away from home</h2>
<p>An emergency may happen while household members are at work, school, or different appointments. Agree on how you will communicate if normal routes or services are disrupted, and choose meeting arrangements suited to your location. Keep important contact information available in a form that does not depend entirely on one phone remaining charged and connected.</p>
<p>Ask schools, care providers, and relevant workplaces how they handle emergencies and reunification. Do not assume you will be allowed to collect someone in the usual way or that every road will remain open. Knowing the existing procedures helps you build a household plan that complements them rather than relying on contradictory assumptions.</p>
<h2>Make records useful rather than merely numerous</h2>
<p>A disorganized collection of document photographs can be difficult to use under stress. Give files clear names, group essential information sensibly, and distinguish current records from outdated copies. Keep originals secure where appropriate. The aim is not to accumulate every possible document but to make necessary information recognizable and recoverable.</p>
<p>For important possessions, a basic inventory can help you describe what you own if you later need to make an insurance claim. Follow your insurer's guidance about useful documentation. Store the inventory separately from the items themselves, and avoid including unnecessary sensitive information in files that other people may access.</p>
<h2>Practice without turning it into a race for objects</h2>
<p>A household practice should focus on safe movement, communication, and understanding the plan. Do not teach children that the exercise is about collecting favorite toys before leaving. Explain clearly that belongings stay behind when there is danger and that adults follow emergency directions too.</p>
<p>Notice practical obstacles during a calm rehearsal: a carrier buried under boxes, an inaccessible contact list, or an exit route blocked by storage. Correct those problems while there is time. If a household member has access needs, include them in planning and identify assistance arrangements that are realistic rather than vaguely promised.</p>
<p>Return to the sentimental question only after safety planning is clear. Ask whether an irreplaceable memory can be copied, recorded, or shared now. You might photograph a handwritten recipe, record a relative explaining an object, or make an additional copy of a family image. These actions preserve meaning without creating an expectation that someone must rescue the original. They also let you enjoy the story today instead of leaving its significance hidden in a drawer.</p>
<h2>Let the question change today's choices</h2>
<p>If you discover that your most valued things are relationships, health, and memories, let that inform ordinary spending and scheduling. The <a href="/category/simple-living/">simple living collection</a> explores those everyday tradeoffs. Save copies now, prepare sensibly, and leave possessions behind whenever safety requires it.</p>
